Quiet luxury: etiquette in a fast world.

With Natalia Langsdale, author of Kindly Advise

Get the book Watch on YouTube Read the transcript

Show notes

Luxury is less, not more. And “we’ll circle back” is worse than no.

Natalia Langsdale has spent three decades on the French Riviera, working across yachting, business aviation and luxury PR. She recorded this conversation in the week her first book, Kindly Advise: Old-School Business Etiquette for a Fast, Rude, Automated World, launched alongside the Monaco Yacht Show.

She explains why luxury is about removing friction rather than adding spectacle, why ultra-high-net-worth clients can detect desperation instantly, how to spot unpaid “exposure” work before you agree to it, and where the line sits between automating your business and automating your relationships.

Three decades on the Riviera

Oliver

Natalia, it’s great to have you on the show. You’ve spent the past three decades on the French Riviera, moving between yachting and business aviation. How did the journey start, and why are you still on it?

Natalia

Thank you very much for having me. I arrived on the French Riviera at the tender age of ten, in 1992, and it’s been a boomerang for me ever since. I’ve left and come back several times: I’ve lived in London and in Auckland, worked in Ireland, and studied in Edinburgh.

My heart has always been here. But professionally it’s also been the most interesting place for me, because of my languages and because of the cosmopolitan mix of the luxury world, which I find fascinating to be part of.

What luxury actually means

Oliver

You’ve been quoted by the Financial Times as a luxury expert, so clearly you know a thing or two about this world. In your own words, what does luxury mean to you and the clients you’ve worked with? And how is that different from the way some brands think of it?

Natalia

For the clients I’ve worked with, luxury is rarely about gold taps and loud logos. It’s about removing friction. It means their time is respected (time is of the essence), their preferences are remembered, and problems are solved quietly and quickly, before they’re even aware there was a problem in the first place.

Many brands think luxury means adding more: more packaging, more adjectives, more theatre. But genuine luxury often means less. Less waiting, less explaining, less chasing, fewer people who need to be involved. Privacy, consistency and ease are far more luxurious than the spectacle we see in the media.

Where brands get it wrong

Oliver

That actually answers another of my questions, which was how brands sometimes get it wrong. Presumably you’d point to examples where they go further than they need to, or are too loud in how they deliver their goods or services?

Natalia

Absolutely. They lead with status, price and exclusivity instead of relevance, trust and competence. Trust is huge when you’re dealing with ultra-high-net-worth individuals. These clients are constantly being sold to, so they can detect desperation almost immediately.

It’s fascinating to watch, especially in yachting. New brokers come in very hungry for the business and don’t respect the rules of the game, the way you deal with these individuals. For me, the strongest approach isn’t to keep telling someone how extraordinary you are, or your brand is, or what you’re selling. It’s to understand their world, make their life easier, and deliver exactly what you promise. And I mean exactly. That comes through in my book, which is out this Friday.

Quiet confidence is the key. It travels much further than a deck that uses the word “exclusive” seventeen times, or a hundred.

Where the book came from

Oliver

Which brings us on to your book. Your first book, Kindly Advise, launches on Friday. It opens with the line that somewhere between the last handwritten letter and the hundredth “just circling back”, business lost its etiquette. When did you first notice that happening?

Natalia

It’s a funny story, or not so funny. I’m a new mum to a two-year-old, so I’d taken three years out, and it was time to get back into the workforce. In the slightly desperate business of finding new clients, I started approaching my old contacts and people across the industries I’d worked in, and I kept being left frustrated by moments where things were just left hanging in the air. That got me thinking: what can I do with this? Maybe I should talk about it. Maybe we’ve forgotten how to talk to people, how to practise basic business etiquette.

So it wasn’t one dramatic moment. It was an accumulation of tiny things: emails left unanswered, meetings arranged and then forgotten, proposals requested urgently and never acknowledged, people disappearing rather than simply saying no. Technology has made communication instantaneous, but somehow it has also made people feel less responsible for communicating. I find that fascinating.

After years of seeing the same behaviour across the industries I’ve worked in, I realised we had started treating basic courtesy as an optional extra. That was really the beginning of the book.

Why “we’ll circle back” is worse than no

Oliver

One of the points you make is that “let’s circle back” is, in a sense, a small act of professional violence. Why is it so much worse than a straight no?

Natalia

Because a no gives you freedom, and “we’ll circle back” keeps you professionally suspended in midair. You don’t know where you stand. You don’t know whether to follow up, wait, decline something else, or quietly accept that everyone involved has moved on except you. It transfers all the uncertainty to the other person while allowing the speaker to feel perfectly polite.

A respectful no may disappoint someone for five minutes, but a vague maybe can waste several weeks. Clarity is far kinder. At least you can move on.

The cost of unpaid exposure work

Oliver

Something else you write about, clearly from first-hand experience, is the cost of unpaid “exposure” work. A lot of our listeners are agency people and brand marketers who see this from both sides. How do you spot it coming, and how do you decline gracefully?

Natalia

It’s nearly always wrapped in some form of enthusiasm. “This will be amazing visibility.” “There could be lots of opportunities later.” Or: “We don’t have a budget at the moment, but…” And that “but” is much bigger than we fear.

Don’t get me wrong: exposure can occasionally be valuable. But it has to be specific. Exposure to whom? In what format? What’s being guaranteed? And what exactly are they asking you to provide?

As for declining, you always have to be polite. I sometimes use a bit of humour, because I feel we’ve lost that, and there’s advice in the book on how to do it politely but with a little piquant, as we say in French. Something like: “Thank you for thinking of me. I’d be delighted to explore this, but I’m unable to commit to the scope without a fee.” Just be honest and clear about your own objectives.

When somebody reaches out, you might be thinking, “Goodness, I need this job,” or “I’ve got nothing else lined up.” Don’t undersell yourself. Don’t assume you’re less capable than you are, or that your work doesn’t come at a cost. Stand up for yourself. Your work already has value, and the fee is simply part of explaining it. Replying, showing up and naming a number are, for me, among the rarest and most valuable things in business.

Why is naming a number so hard, especially in luxury? Because money exposes the reality of the conversation.

Ghosting, rudeness and the human touch

Oliver

There’s another distinction I’d like you to comment on: ghosting people, or simply avoiding a straight answer, versus rudeness. Are they one and the same, or is there a line between them? How do you think about that in luxury generally?

Natalia

A lot of people blame the fact that the world’s moving too fast and we have other priorities. Ghosting can happen, but give it a timeline. Don’t leave somebody hanging for weeks until it quietly turns out it was never meant to be. Not replying is what’s actually rude.

It might take you an extra minute to write one line in an email, or to make a quick phone call, but at least that person knows where they stand, and you can rely on them if you need to come back to them later about something else. You never know; they might need you in a more serious capacity down the line, and they’ll remember: “That person was rude. They didn’t reply, didn’t take the time to discuss what I needed, refused me point-blank, or just left me hanging.” It doesn’t cost you much. Just a minute of your time.

Sadly, it’s also about how we use technology to try to be more efficient, which is something I cover in the book. But is it efficient? Are we losing the content? People write something with AI, don’t bother to check it, send it, and then there’s something horrendous in there they would never have said, or it’s phrased in a way that doesn’t get at what they’re actually trying to say.

We need that human element. A lot of people are trying to get back to it, but time is the constraint that’s blocking it. So I think we just need to take the time. That’s a luxury for our clients and end users, but it matters just as much in how we treat our own people and how we respond to others.

Rudeness as a power move

Oliver

That brings to mind the personal touch, which goes such a long way in business and in life more generally. Luxury can sometimes be perceived as a little snobbish, and people in the space can occasionally be quite rude, whether that’s a function of money and wealth or of never being told no. Do you ever see rudeness used as leverage, as a power move?

Natalia

It’s a valid point, and I think it’s partly cultural. I live in the south of France, with Monaco next door, and I notice that people with a certain appearance can be looked down upon. Whereas (how do I put this politely?) the people with the most money are often the ones who don’t dress up or wear all the brands. Old money versus new money, right? You never know who’s in front of you. So yes, that snobbishness and rudeness exists, and you’d see it more obviously in a shopping district than in yachting or business aviation.

In those industries, you know who you’re dealing with from the outset. It might be the owner’s representative or the PA, but these people are researched so thoroughly, and it’s such a small world, that it’s simple to understand who’s who. When you’re in Louis Vuitton or Chanel, though, you don’t know who’s walking through the door until you actually have a conversation. Or, now, with Google Lens and smart glasses, you can just scan them and immediately get the information, which is scary enough.

But why don’t we just take the time to reflect on how we’d like to be treated? You don’t know who you’re dealing with, so be polite in any situation. Yes, you might be snubbed. But take a deep breath and think, “Maybe I was in that position once.” Take it easy, calmly find out what the situation is, and then assess who the person in front of you is.

What must stay human

Oliver

Kindly Advise is out on Friday, and I really like the subtitle you’ve gone with: Old-School Business Etiquette for a Fast, Rude, Automated World. Where specifically have speed and automation made us worse? And can you think of counter-examples where they’ve improved things?

Natalia

The world has always kept developing, whatever century we’ve been in. Today, automation can handle information, organisation, research, reminders: all that lovely admin. Design, too, is now quite impressive.

But it should support the relationship, not become the relationship. Judgement must stay human. Taste must stay human. Difficult conversations, genuine hospitality, and the ability to understand what someone hasn’t explicitly said must stay human. Even with all that technology, we still need to be the ones feeding it to make it work. We’re not going to be replaced by bots. Well, I hope I won’t be! We still need that feel factor: the ability to read people and catch the nuance.

If a client is trusting you with their time, their privacy, their family’s reputation, or a significant amount of money, as they are in the luxury world, they need to feel that somebody intelligent and accountable is genuinely paying attention. AI can help you prepare the room. A human still needs to be the one welcoming the guest.

Where to draw the line with AI

Oliver

So I guess that’s where you’d draw the line. As luxury brands adopt AI, and it takes over more of the functions that used to be the remit of humans, how would you describe the line between adopting these technologies and keeping the human touch? Where is too far, whether in marketing or in supply chains, and where is it acceptable, or even warranted?

Natalia

Relying on it 100% is certainly too far. I don’t know how much AI you use day to day, but I’ve used it in lots of different ways, and I still have to go back, edit it and put my own touch on it. As I said, it helps speed things up and gain time, and time is of the essence. But when it has to express who you are, or what you’re trying to get across, that’s where the line comes: you have to work together with it.

Technology can’t work by itself, or if it does, it becomes extremely dangerous. That’s making the news at the moment: if we just let it be, it can destroy us. So we have to rein it in and stand over it. Use it as a tool, rather than relying 100% on something when we don’t even know how far it can go.

The launch and the Monaco Yacht Show

Oliver

The book is out on Friday. Where can people get their hands on a copy?

Natalia

The first copy is here in my hands! It’ll be on Amazon, in paperback and on Kindle, from Friday, and then off it goes into the horizon. Hopefully I’ll get plenty of good feedback, and some interesting emails questioning or arguing with what I’m trying to put across.

I’m very excited. It’s launching this week here in Monaco, alongside the Monaco Yacht Show, as a good calling card for me.

Oliver

I think you’ll find a lot of people looking forward to talking to you about it. As you said, the Monaco Yacht Show opens this week. What are you looking forward to, and what are your expectations going in?

Natalia

It’s happening in the most appropriately chaotic way possible. Launching a book during the Monaco Yacht Show could be seen as completely insane. But I like to push buttons and I like to be disruptive. Even my little two-year-old will be helping with the promo, wearing a T-shirt with a QR code on it.

So I’ve got all guns blazing. I’m doing marketing in a way no one’s seen at a Monaco Yacht Show. I’ve been doing yacht shows since 2008, and we’re now in 2026, so that’s a lot of yacht shows, and I’m tired of seeing the same thing done. I want to stand out in my own way by bringing something valuable to people, reminding them that in today’s world we need to pay attention. We need to stay human. We need to connect. We need to laugh. As I say in the book, we’ve forgotten how to enjoy a few minutes having a laugh about something banal.

But it’s serious too, of course. I’ve got some great endorsements from VistaJet, MarineMax and UBS Global Wealth Management Monaco, who have put their word of approval on the book. So it’s not just tongue-in-cheek. It has some serious messages, and I hope you enjoy it.

How she’ll celebrate

Oliver

I can’t wait to get my hands on it. I’ve already earmarked the Kindle copy for myself. When Friday comes, how will you celebrate?

Natalia

I hope there’ll be a glass of champagne, because I’m also speaking to a group of students coming specifically from the University of Genoa to hear my insights. And then I’ll go home to my toddler, who will be completely unimpressed by the concept of a book launch and will probably ask me to open a yogurt.

So, two different worlds colliding beautifully, and a very effective way of staying grounded.

Oliver

Natalia, thank you so much for joining us on Scarce. It’s been an absolute pleasure talking to you about this, and I can’t wait to read it in my own time. Thank you again.

Natalia

Thank you so much, Oliver. It’s been a pleasure.
